Rules we didn't write

Part of the Absa App prepaid electricity case study. Absa, 2016. Lead UX Designer.

Selling electricity in a banking app sounds like a payment with a meter number attached. It wasn’t. Municipalities set the rules, a third-party vendor carried the request, and the bank owned none of it. This is how I turned those rules into screens people could get through in a hurry.

First, I mapped where municipalities differed

Each municipality had its own rules and procedures for selling prepaid electricity, in a store or online. I studied them and mapped where the requirements differed, then sat with business analysts and systems architects to trace each request: what the app sends, which outside service answers, and what comes back. Much of it sat outside the bank’s systems, so the design had to plan for slow and failed replies.

Then each rule got a moment on screen

The earlier web steps listed the rules as notes. In the app, each one became something the customer sees at the moment it matters:

  • The meter must be valid. The number goes to the utility first, so an invalid or blocked meter shows inline on the first screen, with a contact number.
  • The meter’s details belong to the utility. The registered name, address and provider come back and fill in for you. If there’s no address, the field is hidden.
  • Arrears are paid first. Any arrears show before you pay. If your amount is lower, a message warns that you won’t receive any electricity.
  • R30 to R1,000 per purchase. Checked inline as you type.
  • Purchases can’t be reversed. An Important Information step asks you to confirm you’ve read it before you continue.
  • A new recipient needs extra security. Buying for someone new triggers a Surecheck security confirmation.
  • Each municipality sets the receipt. The full receipt holds everything required, while the token screen shows only what you need at the meter.
  • Key-change tokens go in order. They’re numbered, and shared tokens carry the same note.

After: the annotated flow, with each rule tied to a screen and a behaviour.

Designing for a reply we didn't control

Once you confirm, the app has to wait for the municipality’s system to issue the token, and that can take up to 60 seconds. We designed a waiting screen that says exactly that, so a slow reply reads as normal rather than broken. After launch, analytics showed many customers weren’t receiving tokens, and I worked with engineering to make delivery from the provider more reliable.

What it taught me

Rule-heavy products tempt you to push the rules into small print and hope. I’d rather find the moment each rule actually affects the person and put it there, in plain words. It’s the same work I look for now in regulated B2B tools: understand the system first, then design the screens around what it can and can’t promise.
