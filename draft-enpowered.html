From a loan spreadsheet to a tool partners could sell with

EnPowered helps businesses pay for clean energy projects, like solar, out of the savings those projects create. Its partners priced that financing with a dense spreadsheet, which was slow and hard to explain to customers. As the sole UX/UI designer, I took the tool from that spreadsheet to a Typeform wizard and then to an in-house platform where partners build an estimate, send a branded proposal and collect the financing application in one place. Partners went from wrestling with a spreadsheet to accurate estimates in minutes, and proposal adoption went up over the first six months [ASK RYAN: the number for proposal adoption, before vs after].

Where it ended up: the financing application, three steps from start to submit.

Partners were quoting solar loans from a spreadsheet

Before the Financing Accelerator, a quote meant filling in EnPowered's Solar Loan Calculator. It was accurate. It handled tax credits, depreciation, downpayments and cost of capital. But it was a wall of yellow cells, and partners struggled to use it with their customers. Our goal was a tool partners could run on their own, from first estimate to final application.

The starting point. Notice the ITC and depreciation rows: the next version couldn't hold them.

A Typeform wizard proved the idea fast

We started cheap. A Typeform wizard asked one question at a time and produced a clean, automated proposal at the end. It was a big step up from the spreadsheet. We ran calls with partners and watched the usage data, and the feedback told us the direction was right.

Typeform couldn't do the maths, so we built our own

The wizard's success showed its limits. Typeform had no easy way to adjust interest, account for depreciation or handle the other variables the spreadsheet already managed. We could keep bending a form tool, or own the product. We chose to build an in-house, cloud-based version. The first one kept the wizard flow partners had validated, now in EnPowered's brand, with room for detail like when an incentive is applied. A wizard is good at starting and poor at editing

Partners needed a view of the whole project (later called a deal) and a way to jump between sections without losing context. A linear wizard couldn't give them either. I mapped a new structure around the deal itself, with a project list and a summary page as the hubs. Then I sketched several layouts and kept the simplest: the wizard's sections as tabs down the left. Read the full story of the wizard-to-tabs shift.

The IA map. Share, export, preview and request financing all hang off the project list and summary pages.

Early sketch of the left-hand tabs, with an info panel beside the form.

Help where partners get stuck, with a way to switch it off

Fields like cost of capital or a utility cost escalator mean little to someone new to financing. In sketches I explored a helper-text toggle, tooltips that show when a field is active, an intro modal on what gives a project its best chance of funding, and an offer to fill a new project with example data. Each idea came with an off switch, so experienced partners weren't slowed down. [ASK RYAN: which of these shipped?]

Estimate, proposal and application in one place

The Financing Accelerator puts the whole deal behind a few tabs. Partners enter project details, see a real-time summary of the financial results, and download a branded proposal as a PDF or the payment schedules as a CSV. When the customer is ready, the partner shares a secure link to the financing application or completes it on their behalf. That replaced the email chains and file attachments.

Cost & Savings: inputs at the top, first-year estimates and the payment schedule right below.

Partners could quote, propose and apply on their own

We were a startup and didn't track everything, so most of the evidence is qualitative. Over about six months, partners went from needing a spreadsheet and our help to generating accurate estimates in minutes [ASK RYAN: the number for time per estimate, before vs after]. More of them sent proposals, and customers completed financing applications through the tool instead of by email.
